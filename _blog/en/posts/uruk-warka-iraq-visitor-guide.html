Long before Babylon, Athens or Rome, there was Uruk. On the plain of southern Iraq, where the Euphrates once flowed, a cluster of villages grew into what many historians call the first true city in the world, and the place where people first began to write. If you plan to visit Uruk (Warka) in Iraq, this guide explains where the site lies, why it matters so much, what you can see among its mounds today and how it fits into a journey through ancient Mesopotamia.

Uruk is not a site of tall standing columns. It is a vast landscape of sun-baked mud brick, pottery shards and weathered temple platforms stretching to the horizon. What makes it unforgettable is the story beneath your feet: here, more than five thousand years ago, people organised a city of temples, workshops and offices, kept accounts on clay tablets and told stories of a king named Gilgamesh.

Where is Uruk?

The ruins of Uruk, known locally as Warka, lie in Al-Muthanna province in southern Iraq, east of the city of Samawah and roughly 300 km south-east of Baghdad by road. In antiquity the city stood on a branch of the Euphrates; the river has since shifted its course, which is why the site now rises from an open, almost desert-like plain.

In the Bible the city appears as Erech, one of the first cities named in the Book of Genesis. Some scholars have also suggested that the Arabic name of the country, al-Iraq, may echo the ancient name of Uruk, although this is one theory among several.

The first city in the world

From villages to a metropolis

Settlement at Uruk goes back to the fifth millennium BCE. During the so-called Uruk period, roughly 4000 to 3100 BCE, it grew at an extraordinary pace. By the beginning of the third millennium BCE the walled city covered around 5.5 square kilometres, making it the largest known city of its age. Archaeologists believe tens of thousands of people lived here, working as farmers, herders, potters, weavers, scribes and priests.

Where writing began

Uruk's most famous gift to humanity is writing. In the temple district of Eanna, excavators found thousands of clay tablets from the late fourth millennium BCE, among the earliest written documents known anywhere. They are mostly practical records: rations, livestock, grain and lists of words. From these simple signs pressed into clay grew cuneiform, the script used across the Near East for some three thousand years. A modest sign at the site today reminds visitors that the first written words started here.

The city of Gilgamesh

According to the Sumerian King List, Gilgamesh was a king of Uruk, and the Epic of Gilgamesh, one of the oldest works of literature in the world, opens and closes with praise of the city's mighty walls. Those walls are real: archaeologists have traced a circuit about 9 km long around the ancient city. Whether or not Gilgamesh built them, standing on their line you are walking in the setting of the world's first great epic.

What to see at Uruk today

The Eanna precinct of Inanna

Eanna, the "House of Heaven", was the sanctuary of Inanna, goddess of love and war, later known as Ishtar. Over many centuries temples were built, demolished and rebuilt here on top of one another. Some walls and columns were decorated with thousands of small clay cones, their coloured heads pressed into the plaster to form zigzag and diamond patterns. Today the most impressive part of Eanna is the later ziggurat mound, still rising above the plain with its mud-brick core and the regular holes left by ancient construction.

The Anu ziggurat and the White Temple

In the district of the sky god Anu stood a high terrace topped by the White Temple, named after the white plaster that once covered its walls. Built in the late fourth millennium BCE, it is often described as a forerunner of the stepped ziggurats you will later see at Ur. Climb the mound and look out over the endless plain: it is easy to imagine how this shining building would have been visible from far away.

Later temples and the city wall

Uruk remained a holy city long after its political power faded. In the Seleucid and Parthian periods, after Alexander the Great, temples such as the Bit Resh were still built and used. Along the edge of the site you can follow traces of the great city wall, and everywhere the ground is strewn with fragments of pottery.

Treasures from Uruk in museums

Many of Uruk's finest objects were found during German excavations that began in 1912 and continued, with interruptions, until 1989. Two of the best known are kept in the Iraq Museum in Baghdad: the Warka Vase, an alabaster vessel carved with a procession of offerings to Inanna, and the Mask of Warka, a beautifully carved marble face of a woman often called the "Lady of Uruk". Reconstructed cone-mosaic columns from Eanna are kept in the Vorderasiatisches Museum in Berlin. A visit to the Iraq Museum before or after Uruk brings the site to life.

How to get to Uruk

Uruk is reached by road. Samawah is the nearest city, and the site is also within reach of Najaf to the north-west and Nasiriyah to the south-east, so it fits naturally into a drive through southern Iraq. The last part of the approach runs across a flat, open landscape. Facilities at the site are very limited, and the area is large, so travelling with a guide and driver who know the site makes the visit far easier and more meaningful.

Best time to visit Uruk

The best season is from late October to March, when the days are mild and the light is clear. In winter mornings can be chilly in the open desert, so bring a warm layer. Summer in southern Iraq is extremely hot, and there is almost no shade at Uruk, so a summer visit is only sensible very early in the morning.

Practical tips for your visit

  • Wear sturdy shoes: the ground is uneven, and the mounds are made of loose earth and brick.
  • Bring a hat, sunglasses, sunscreen and plenty of water, as there is no shade and few facilities.
  • Allow about one and a half to two hours to walk between Eanna and the Anu ziggurat.
  • Do not climb on fragile walls and never take pottery shards or bricks from the site.
  • Read a little about the Epic of Gilgamesh before you go: it makes the walls and temples speak.
  • Entry and visa rules for Iraq change from time to time, so check the current requirements for your nationality before you travel.

Uruk on your Iraq itinerary
