Arriving at the Imam Hussein Shrine in Karbala is an experience that few travellers forget. A golden dome rises above the rooftops, two gilded minarets frame it against the sky, and from early morning until long after midnight a steady stream of visitors flows through its gates. If you plan to visit Karbala in Iraq, this guide covers the shrine's story, what to look for inside the complex, how to get there from Baghdad and the etiquette that makes a visit easy and respectful.

For Shia Muslims the shrine is among the most sacred places in the world: it holds the tomb of Imam Hussein ibn Ali (peace be upon him), grandson of the Prophet Muhammad and son of Imam Ali and Fatima al-Zahra. For travellers of any faith, it is also a remarkable piece of Islamic architecture and the beating heart of a city whose whole identity grew up around a single grave.

Where is the Imam Hussein Shrine?

The shrine stands in the centre of Karbala, a city on the edge of the desert west of the Euphrates, roughly 100 km south-west of Baghdad. By car the journey usually takes around one and a half to two hours, depending on traffic and checkpoints. Najaf is about 80 km further south, and the ruins of Babylon lie a short drive to the east, so Karbala sits right at the centre of Iraq's classic heritage route.

A few hundred metres to the north-east rises a second golden dome: the shrine of al-Abbas, Imam Hussein's half-brother. The wide pedestrian esplanade that links the two sanctuaries is called Bayn al-Haramayn, "between the two shrines", and walking it is one of the defining moments of any visit to Karbala.

A short history of the shrine

Karbala in 680 CE

In the year 61 AH (680 CE), Imam Hussein travelled from Medina towards Kufa with members of his family and a small group of companions. On the plain of Karbala, beside the Euphrates, they were stopped by a much larger army. On the tenth day of the month of Muharram, the day known as Ashura, Imam Hussein and his companions were killed. For Muslims, and above all for Shia Muslims, his stand has become a timeless symbol of principle, sacrifice and refusal to accept injustice. He was buried where he fell, and the place soon became a destination for visitors.

The first buildings

According to tradition, the first structure over the grave was a simple building of brick and plaster, raised by local people of the Banu Asad tribe or in the time of al-Mukhtar al-Thaqafi a few years after the battle. Under the early Abbasids a dome and gates were added, and the shrine was rebuilt and extended several times over the following centuries.

From the Buyids to the Qajars

A decisive phase came in the late 10th century, when the Buyid ruler 'Adud al-Dawla carried out a major building programme between about 977 and 982 CE. The dome he raised over the tomb forms the core of the sanctuary you see today. In the 14th century the Jalayirid rulers completed the Golden Iwan, the great open hall facing the courtyard. Safavid patrons later added a new screen around the tomb and a carved wooden sarcophagus.

The golden look of Karbala belongs largely to the Qajar era. In the 1790s Agha Mohammad Khan Qajar ordered the dome to be rebuilt and covered in gold, and his successor Fath-Ali Shah commissioned a new tomb screen. Gilding of the iwan and minarets followed, and the dome was regilded several times in later generations.

What to see inside the complex

The golden dome and minarets

The outer dome rises about 27 metres and is entirely sheathed in gold, with a ring of twelve windows around its base. Flanked by its two gilded minarets, it glows warm amber in the late afternoon and shines brilliantly under the floodlights at night — the moment most photographers wait for.

The courtyard and the gates

The shrine is enclosed by a wall pierced by ten gates, their wooden doors decorated with glass and tilework. Behind them, two storeys of arched rooms line a broad courtyard; for centuries these rooms housed students and scholars. The best-known entrance is the Qibla Gate, also called the Golden Gate (Bab al-Dhahab). Look up at the tile panels above the arches: deep blues, turquoise and saffron yellow, with Qur'anic verses and floral patterns that change with the light.

The sanctuary and the zarih

Inside the sanctuary, walls and ceilings sparkle with mirror mosaic that multiplies the light of the chandeliers. The tomb itself is enclosed in a zarih, a richly worked screen of silver and gold. The current one was crafted in Qom from gold, silver, copper, brass and teak, and was unveiled in 2013 after years of work by master craftsmen.

Those buried beside the Imam

Imam Hussein does not rest alone. According to tradition, his son Ali al-Akbar is buried at his feet, and the companions who fell with him lie together nearby within the sanctuary. Close to the entrance is the tomb of Habib ibn Mazahir al-Asadi, an elderly companion of the Imam who is especially loved by visitors.

Bayn al-Haramayn and the shrine of al-Abbas

Leaving the shrine through its north-eastern gates you step onto Bayn al-Haramayn, the open, paved promenade that leads to the shrine of al-Abbas ibn Ali. Al-Abbas was Imam Hussein's half-brother and standard-bearer, remembered for his loyalty and courage. Walking slowly between the two golden domes, especially in the evening when families stroll and the lights come on, is one of the most memorable things you can do in Iraq. Allow time for both sanctuaries: they are part of the same visit and form a single spiritual landscape.

How to get to Karbala from Baghdad

Most travellers reach Karbala by road from Baghdad. A private car with driver is the most comfortable option, since it lets you combine Karbala with Babylon the same day or continue on to Najaf. Visitors coming from the south can also use Najaf's international airport, about an hour and a half away by road. Near the shrines, the streets are largely pedestrian, so expect to walk the last part on foot through the busy market streets — which is part of the charm.

Best time to visit

The most pleasant months are October to April, when days are warm and evenings mild. Summer temperatures are very high, so plan visits for early morning or after sunset. Whatever the season, the hour before sunset and the evening are the best times: the gold turns soft, the courtyards fill with families and the atmosphere becomes calm and intimate.

Karbala hosts some of the largest religious gatherings in the world, above all during Muharram and on Arbaeen, forty days after Ashura, when millions of pilgrims arrive — many on foot from Najaf and other cities. Witnessing these days is deeply moving, but the city is extremely crowded. If you prefer a quieter visit, ask your guide about the calendar before setting your dates.

Practical tips and etiquette

  • Dress modestly: men wear long trousers and sleeves; women cover their hair and wear a long, loose abaya. Your guide can help you arrange one.
  • Shoes: leave them at the shoe depositories by the gates and collect them with your token when you leave.
  • Security checks: bags are checked at the entrances to the shrine area. Travel light and leave large bags in the car or hotel.
  • Photography: rules can change; phones are usually fine in the courtyards and on Bayn al-Haramayn, but always ask before photographing people, and be discreet inside the sanctuary.
  • Non-Muslim visitors: respectful visitors are welcomed in the courtyards, and going with a local guide makes entrances and customs easier to navigate.
  • Visa and entry: requirements change from time to time, so check the current rules for your nationality before you travel.
