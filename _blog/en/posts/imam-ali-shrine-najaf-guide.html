Few places in Iraq leave as deep an impression as the Imam Ali Shrine in Najaf. Rising above the old city, its golden dome and twin gilded minarets catch the desert light from far away, and day and night the courtyards are full of pilgrims who have travelled from across Iraq and the wider world. If you are planning to visit Najaf, this guide explains the shrine's history, what you will see inside the complex, how to get there from Baghdad and the etiquette that makes a visit go smoothly.

For Shia Muslims this is one of the holiest places on earth, the resting place of Imam Ali ibn Abi Talib (peace be upon him), cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet Muhammad. For travellers of every background it is also one of the great monuments of Islamic architecture, shaped over more than a thousand years by Abbasid, Buyid, Safavid and later patrons.

Where is the Imam Ali Shrine?

The shrine stands at the heart of Najaf, a city on the edge of the western desert, roughly 160 km south of Baghdad. The drive usually takes around two and a half to three hours. Najaf also has its own international airport, which receives flights from several countries in the region.

Just 10 km away lies Kufa, the city Imam Ali made his capital, and on the northern edge of Najaf stretches Wadi al-Salam, the "Valley of Peace", one of the largest cemeteries in the world. Together, Najaf and Kufa form one of the most meaningful days of any heritage journey through Iraq.

The history of the shrine

A hidden grave

Imam Ali was struck while leading the dawn prayer in the Great Mosque of Kufa during Ramadan and passed away a few days later, in the year 40 AH (661 CE). According to tradition, his family buried him at night, outside the city and in secret, so that the grave would be protected. For more than a century its location was known only to his descendants and a small circle of followers.

The gazelle of Harun al-Rashid

A well-known story tells how the grave came to light. The Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid was hunting near Kufa when a gazelle took refuge on a small rise; his hounds and falcons refused to follow it there. When the caliph asked about the place, he was told it was the grave of Ali ibn Abi Talib. Around 786 CE he ordered the first building over the tomb, a modest structure with a dome and four entrances.

Buyids, Safavids and the golden dome

In the late 10th century the Buyid ruler 'Adud al-Dawla built a far grander shrine and endowed the growing town around it; he himself chose to be buried inside the complex. The famous Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta, who came in 1327, described silk carpets and lamps of gold and silver.

The shrine you see today largely takes its form from the Safavid period in the 17th century, when the great courtyard was laid out according to a design attributed to the scholar and architect Sheikh Baha'i. In 1743 Nader Shah ordered the dome and minarets to be covered in gilded copper plates, replacing the earlier blue and green tiles, and creating the golden silhouette that has become the symbol of Najaf.

What to see inside the complex

The great courtyard

The shrine sits in the middle of a vast open courtyard framed by two storeys of pointed arches, faced with blue, turquoise and yellow tilework. In past centuries the small rooms behind these arches housed students of Najaf's religious seminary, one of the oldest centres of learning in the Islamic world. In the evening, when the lights come on and families sit on carpets in the courtyard, the atmosphere is calm and unforgettable.

The Clock Gate and the other portals

Most visitors enter from the east through the Clock Gate, crowned by a tiled clock tower. Its clock and bells were made in Manchester, England, and installed in 1887 — a surprising detail for British visitors. Other entrances carry the names of the scholar Sheikh al-Tusi and of Muslim ibn Aqil, the cousin of Imam Hussein.

The Golden Iwan

Facing the courtyard on the east side of the sanctuary is the Golden Iwan, a monumental open hall entirely covered in gold and flanked by the two gilded minarets. Persian verses praising Imam Ali were inscribed here in gold in the 18th century.

The sanctuary

Inside, the tomb chamber glitters with thousands of pieces of mirror mosaic that reflect the light of the chandeliers. The tomb itself is enclosed in a richly worked silver screen, called a zarih, crafted by Indian artisans and unveiled in 1942. According to Shia tradition, the Prophets Adam and Noah are also buried here, beside Imam Ali.

How to get to Najaf from Baghdad

Most travellers reach Najaf by road from Baghdad, often combining the journey with Babylon or Karbala. A private car with driver is the most comfortable option, as it lets you stop on the way and arrive at the shrine at a good time of day. If your trip starts in the south, Najaf's airport is a practical alternative. From the city centre, the shrine is reached on foot through the old covered market, which leads straight to the eastern gate.

Best time to visit

The most comfortable months are October to April, when the days are warm but not hot. Summer in Najaf is very hot, so plan visits for early morning or after sunset. Evenings are particularly beautiful: the dome glows in the floodlights and the courtyards are at their liveliest.

Major religious anniversaries draw very large crowds. They are deeply moving to witness, but if you prefer a quieter visit, ask your guide about the calendar before fixing your dates.

Practical tips and etiquette

  • Dress modestly: men in long trousers and sleeves; women cover their hair and wear a long, loose robe (abaya). Your guide can help you arrange one.
  • Shoes: you leave them at the shoe depositories near the entrances before stepping into the courtyard and collect them with a token on the way out.
  • Security: there are bag checks at the gates. Travel light and leave large bags in the car or hotel.
  • Photography: rules can change; phones are usually fine in the courtyards, but always ask before photographing people, and be discreet inside the sanctuary.
  • Non-Muslim visitors: respectful visitors are welcomed in the courtyards, and going with a local guide makes everything easier.
  • Visa and entry: rules change from time to time, so check the current requirements for your nationality before you travel.
