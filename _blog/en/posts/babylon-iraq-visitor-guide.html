Most travellers know Babylon from stories long before they ever see it: the Tower of Babel, the Hanging Gardens, King Nebuchadnezzar, the city where Alexander the Great died. What surprises almost everyone is how easy it is to actually visit Babylon in Iraq today. The ruins lie about 85 km south of Baghdad, and in a single morning you can walk the Processional Way and stand beside brick reliefs of bulls and dragons that were laid more than 2,500 years ago.

This guide covers what to see at Babylon, how to get there from Baghdad, how much time to allow and when to go, with the practical advice I give the travellers I take to the site.

Where is Babylon?

The ruins of Babylon sit in Babil Province in central Iraq, on the banks of the Euphrates and just outside the modern city of Hilla. From Baghdad it is roughly 85 km south, around an hour and a half by car depending on traffic.

In 2019 UNESCO added Babylon to its World Heritage List, recognising a city that for centuries was one of the largest and most influential places in the ancient world. The site is large and open, with original walls, excavated areas and modern reconstructions standing side by side.

A short history of Babylon

Hammurabi and the first great age

Babylon was already an important town at the start of the second millennium BC, but it became a major capital under Hammurabi, who ruled in the 18th century BC. He is remembered above all for his law code, carved on a black stone stele that is now displayed in the Louvre in Paris.

Nebuchadnezzar II and the city you see today

Babylon's most famous era came more than a thousand years later, under the Neo-Babylonian kings, and above all Nebuchadnezzar II (reigned 605–562 BC). Most of what visitors see belongs to his building programme: the massive city walls, the royal palaces, the Ishtar Gate and the Processional Way. Many bricks on the site are still stamped with cuneiform inscriptions bearing his name.

Persians, Greeks and a slow fade

In 539 BC the city passed peacefully to Cyrus the Great of Persia. The famous Cyrus Cylinder, a clay cylinder describing his entry into Babylon, was found here in 1879 and is now in the British Museum in London. In 323 BC Alexander the Great died in Babylon, having planned to make it a capital of his empire. After that the city slowly lost its importance and was eventually abandoned, its mud-brick buildings sinking back into the plain.

What to see at Babylon

The Ishtar Gate and its original reliefs

The Ishtar Gate was the grand northern entrance to the inner city, dedicated to the goddess Ishtar. Its most spectacular part, covered in blue glazed bricks, was excavated by a German team led by Robert Koldewey in the early 20th century and rebuilt in Berlin's Pergamon Museum. At the entrance to the site you will find a smaller modern replica that gives a sense of the original colours.

The real highlight is less obvious: the original foundations of the gate are still in place. Walk down between the walls and you will see rows of unglazed brick reliefs of bulls and mušḫuššu, the serpent-dragon sacred to the god Marduk, exactly where Nebuchadnezzar's craftsmen set them.

The Processional Way

From the Ishtar Gate ran the ceremonial road along which statues of the gods were carried during the New Year festival, known as Akitu. Its walls were once lined with glazed lions, the symbol of Ishtar. Today you can walk a section of it and still see stretches of the ancient paving.

The Lion of Babylon

The most photographed monument on the site is a large basalt lion standing over a human figure. Scholars still debate its date and meaning, but for centuries it has been the emblem of Babylon and one of the best-known images of Iraq.

Nebuchadnezzar's Southern Palace

The royal palace, with its courtyards and throne room, was largely reconstructed in the 1980s on top of the original foundations. Look closely at the walls and you can tell the ancient bricks, some inscribed, from the modern ones. It is the best place to grasp the sheer scale of the city.

Etemenanki, the "Tower of Babel"

South of the palace stood Etemenanki, the great ziggurat of Marduk, which many historians link to the biblical story of the Tower of Babel. Only the outline of its base survives, and it often holds water after rain. There is little to see, but it is worth pausing to picture a tower that ancient sources describe as around 90 metres high.

The Hanging Gardens: legend or reality?

The Hanging Gardens of Babylon are one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, yet no firm archaeological evidence for them has been found at Babylon. Some scholars suggest they may have stood in another city, possibly Nineveh. Local guides point out a few candidate spots near the palace; it makes for a good conversation on site, as long as you know the debate is still open.

How to get to Babylon from Baghdad

There is no convenient public transport for tourists to the site itself, so the easiest options are a private car with a driver or an organised tour. The drive from Baghdad takes about an hour and a half. You will pass through several routine checkpoints along the way, which are normal in Iraq; keep your passport within reach.

Travelling with a local guide makes the day simpler: tickets, checkpoints and timing are taken care of, and you arrive in the best light of the day.

Best time to visit Babylon

The ideal season runs from October to April, when days are mild and sunny. From June to September temperatures often pass 45 °C and walking around an open site becomes tiring, so if you travel in summer, arrive early in the morning.

Allow two to three hours on site. A day trip from Baghdad is the most common way to visit, and it leaves time to return to the capital for dinner, or to continue south towards Karbala and Najaf.

Practical tips for your visit

  • Tickets: bought at the site entrance. Prices can change, so carry enough cash in Iraqi dinars.
  • What to wear: comfortable shoes, a hat and water, even in spring. Modest clothing (shoulders and knees covered) is always appreciated.
  • Photography: generally allowed across the site. Morning light is best for the reliefs of the Ishtar Gate foundations.
  • Visa: entry rules for Iraq change from time to time, so always check the latest official information for your nationality before you book.
  • Bring a guide: there are few information panels on site, and without someone to explain each wall, many details are easy to miss.
