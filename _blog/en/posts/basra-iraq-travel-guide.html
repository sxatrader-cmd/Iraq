Basra is where Iraq opens towards the sea. The Tigris and the Euphrates have joined to form the broad Shatt al-Arab, date palms line the banks for kilometres, and the city that grew up here has been a port, a school of scholars and a crossroads of peoples for almost fourteen centuries. If you are planning to visit Basra in Iraq, this guide explains what makes the "gateway of the south" special, what to see in a day or two, and how the city fits into a journey through the Land Between the Rivers.

Many travellers know Basra only as a name on the map, or from the tales of Sinbad the Sailor. Those who come here find a warm, relaxed southern city with a riverside promenade, a fine museum, carved wooden balconies in the old quarter and some of the best dates and fish in the country.

Where is Basra?

Basra lies in the far south of Iraq, on the west bank of the Shatt al-Arab, roughly 545 km south-east of Baghdad by road and not far from the head of the Gulf. The river is formed about 75 km to the north, at Qurna, where the Tigris and the Euphrates finally meet, and it flows on past Basra towards the sea. The city is the capital of Basra province and the country's main port region.

The landscape is flat, green and watery: canals, palm gardens and the river shape daily life here far more than in the dry centre and north of Iraq. Basra is also the natural base for visiting Qurna, the Mesopotamian Marshes and the Sumerian city of Ur.

A short history of Basra

A city founded in the 7th century

Basra was founded in the early years of Islam, around 636 CE, by Utba ibn Ghazwan, a companion of the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him), as a camp on the edge of the desert near the waterways of southern Iraq. It quickly grew into one of the great cities of the early Islamic world. The original site lay a little to the south-west of the present centre, near today's town of al-Zubayr; over the centuries the city moved closer to the river.

A capital of learning

For several centuries Basra was a leading centre of Arabic language, literature and science. The grammarians of Basra, among them al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi, who compiled one of the first Arabic dictionaries and described the metres of Arabic poetry, and his famous student Sibawayh, laid the foundations of Arabic grammar. The writer al-Jahiz, known for his witty essays and his "Book of Animals", was born here, as was the great scientist Ibn al-Haytham, whose work on light and vision is still celebrated by historians of science. Basra was also home to early mystics and preachers such as Hasan al-Basri and Rabi'a al-Adawiyya.

Merchants, ships and Sinbad

Thanks to its position between the rivers and the sea, Basra became a meeting point for ships and caravans travelling between Iraq, Persia, India and East Africa. Spices, textiles, pearls and dates passed through its markets. It is no surprise that the storytellers of the Thousand and One Nights made Basra the port from which Sinbad the Sailor set out on his voyages. Over the centuries Arabs, Persians, Indians, Armenians and many others settled here, and the city's churches, mosques and old merchant houses still reflect that mixed heritage.

What to see in Basra

The Shatt al-Arab Corniche

The heart of Basra is its riverside promenade along the Shatt al-Arab. In the late afternoon families come out to walk, children play, small boats cross the river and tea sellers set up by the water. The sunset here, with the sky turning gold and pink behind the palm trees, is one of the most beautiful moments of any trip to southern Iraq. On the Corniche stands the statue of Badr Shakir al-Sayyab, the Basra-born poet who is considered one of the pioneers of modern Arabic free verse, looking out over the river that inspired so much of his work.

Basra Museum

The Basra Museum is set in a large former palace on the banks of the Shatt al-Arab and opened its first gallery in 2016. Its halls take you through the history of the south: objects from Sumer, Babylon and Assyria, and a gallery devoted to Basra itself and its Islamic past, including coins, pottery and inscriptions. The building, with its terraces facing the river, is worth seeing in its own right, and the museum gives excellent background before you visit Ur and Uruk.

Old Basra and the shanasheel houses

In the older quarters near the al-Ashar and al-Khandaq canals you can still find traditional houses with shanasheel: projecting balconies of carved and latticed wood, often painted, that let the breeze in and keep the sun out while allowing the women of the house to look out unseen. Basra was once called the "Venice of the East" because of its canals, and walking these lanes gives you an idea of how the merchant city looked a century ago. Several of these houses are being restored, and the old city is slowly coming back to life.

The Armenian Church of the Holy Virgin Mary

This peaceful church is a reminder of the Armenian community that has lived in Basra for generations, alongside many other communities. Its simple, dignified interior and quiet courtyard tell another chapter of the city's story as a place where different peoples met and traded.

Markets, dates and fish

The traditional bazaars around al-Ashar are full of spices, perfumes, fabrics, sweets and, above all, dates. Southern Iraq was for centuries one of the great date-growing regions of the world, and Basra varieties such as Barhi are famous across the Gulf. Try fresh fish from the river and the Gulf, and if you have the chance, taste dates straight from the palm in autumn.

How to get to Basra

  • By air: Basra International Airport has domestic flights from Baghdad and Erbil and regional connections, often via the Gulf or Istanbul. Schedules change, so check the current timetable before you book.
  • By road from Baghdad: the drive is long, roughly 545 km, and is best broken up with stops at Babylon, Najaf or Uruk on the way, as our tours do.
  • Getting around: distances inside the city are short by car, but the heat in the warm months makes a driver far more comfortable than walking long distances.
  • Entry requirements: visa rules for Iraq change from time to time and depend on your nationality, so always check the latest official information before travelling.
  • Dress and etiquette: modest clothing is appreciated, especially at churches, mosques and in the markets; ask before photographing people.

Best time to visit Basra

Basra is one of the hottest cities in the region in summer, so the best months to visit are from late October to March, when days are pleasant and evenings by the river can even be cool. Autumn is date-harvest season, and winter brings clear light that is perfect for photography on the Shatt al-Arab. Spring is warm and green, but by May the temperatures rise quickly.
