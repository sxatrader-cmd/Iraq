Long before pyramids rose on the Nile, the Sumerians of southern Mesopotamia were stacking millions of bricks into stepped temple mountains. The finest one still standing is the Great Ziggurat of Ur, near Nasiriyah in southern Iraq. Visiting the Ziggurat of Ur means walking up to a monument more than 4,000 years old, in the city that the Bible remembers as Ur of the Chaldees, the hometown of Abraham.

In this guide you will learn where the ziggurat is, who built it and why, what else to see in ancient Ur, and how to fit it into your journey through Iraq, with the tips I share with every group I bring here.

Where is the Ziggurat of Ur?

The ruins of Ur lie at Tell al-Muqayyar, which means "the mound of bitumen", in Dhi Qar province. The site is about 15 km southwest of the city of Nasiriyah, on a wide, flat plain west of the Euphrates. In ancient times the river and the marshy shore of the Gulf were much closer, and Ur was a busy port and trading city. Over the centuries the river changed its course, the land dried out, and the city was finally abandoned around the 4th century BC.

Today the ziggurat appears on the horizon long before you reach it: a huge block of golden-brown brick rising out of the desert, with nothing around it but sky. It is one of those places where the scale of the landscape makes the monument feel even bigger.

The history of the Great Ziggurat of Ur

Built by King Ur-Nammu around 2100 BC

The ziggurat was begun around 2100 BC by King Ur-Nammu, the founder of the Third Dynasty of Ur, a time when Ur was the capital of a powerful Sumerian state. The work was completed by his son, King Shulgi. Many of the baked bricks are still stamped with Ur-Nammu's name, a royal signature you can sometimes spot in the walls.

A stairway to the moon god

A ziggurat is not a tomb and not a pyramid. It is a raised temple platform, built in stages, with a shrine on the very top. The Ziggurat of Ur was dedicated to Nanna, the Sumerian moon god (known as Sin in Akkadian), the patron of the city. Priests climbed the staircases to the sanctuary at the summit, closer to the sky, while the city spread out below around its temples, palaces and houses.

Restored by Nabonidus

Some 1,500 years after Ur-Nammu, the ziggurat had crumbled. In the 6th century BC the Neo-Babylonian king Nabonidus, the last king of Babylon and a devoted follower of the moon god, restored it. His inscribed clay cylinders, placed in the corners of the building, were found by explorers in the 1850s and helped identify the site as ancient Ur.

Rediscovered by archaeologists

The mound was described by William Loftus in 1850, but Ur became world famous thanks to Sir Leonard Woolley, who led a joint expedition of the British Museum and the Penn Museum from 1922 to 1934. His team uncovered the ziggurat, the temple quarter, residential streets and the spectacular Royal Cemetery. In the 1980s the façade of the lowest stage and the great front staircase were partially reconstructed, which is why the lower part looks so sharp and complete today.

What to see at the ziggurat

The massive base

The base measures roughly 64 by 45 metres. Archaeologists estimate the original height at more than 30 metres, although only the lower parts survive. The core is solid mud brick, wrapped in a thick skin of baked brick set in bitumen, the natural asphalt that gave the site its Arabic name.

Three monumental staircases

The front of the ziggurat is defined by three great stairways: one in the centre and two running along the façade, all meeting at a gateway on the first terrace. Standing at the foot of the central stair and looking up is the classic moment of any visit.

Clever ancient engineering

Look closely at the walls and you will notice narrow vertical slots. These "weeper holes" let moisture escape from the mud-brick core, so the building would not crack from the inside. Woolley also noticed that the walls lean slightly inwards and that the lines are gently curved, a trick that makes the structure look even more powerful from below.

More to explore in ancient Ur

The Royal Cemetery

Next to the temple area lies the Royal Cemetery, where Woolley's team found the tombs of Sumerian kings and queens, including Queen Puabi. Their treasures, such as the Standard of Ur, the Great Lyre and the famous "Ram in a Thicket", are today shared between the Iraq Museum in Baghdad, the British Museum and the Penn Museum. On site you can see the shafts and brick chambers where they were discovered.

Enheduanna and the house of the high priestess

Ur was also home to Enheduanna, daughter of King Sargon of Akkad, who served as high priestess of Nanna here around the 23rd century BC. She wrote hymns to the gods and is the first author in history known to us by name. The remains of the giparu, the residence of the high priestesses, lie close to the ziggurat.

The House of Abraham

In the old residential quarter, among streets of Old Babylonian houses, one restored building is traditionally called the House of Abraham. According to tradition, the Prophet Abraham was born in Ur of the Chaldees, and for Jewish, Christian and Muslim visitors alike this gives the site a special, reflective atmosphere. Nearby are the brick mausolea of the kings Shulgi and Amar-Sin.

Since 2016 Ur has been part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site "The Ahwar of Southern Iraq", together with Uruk, Eridu and the Mesopotamian Marshes.

How to get to Ur from Baghdad

Ur is in the far south, so most travellers visit it on a multi-day trip. From Baghdad to Nasiriyah it is around 360 km by road, roughly five hours depending on stops. From Nasiriyah the site is a short drive. From Basra the journey takes around two to two and a half hours. The most rewarding plan is to sleep in Nasiriyah and combine Ur with the Mesopotamian Marshes at Chibayish and with Basra and Qurna. Allow at least two hours for the ziggurat, the Royal Cemetery and the House of Abraham.

Best time to visit the Ziggurat of Ur

The best season is from October to April, when days are warm and sunny but comfortable. Summers in southern Iraq are extremely hot, and there is almost no shade on the site. Come early in the morning or in the late afternoon: the low sun turns the bricks deep gold against a clear blue sky, and the photographs are at their best.

Practical tips for visiting Ur

  • Bring plenty of water, a hat and sunscreen; there is little shade and few facilities on the site.
  • Wear sturdy, comfortable shoes for walking on uneven ground and brick paths.
  • Rules about climbing the staircase can change, so always follow the instructions of the site guards.
  • Do not take bricks or pottery shards, however small; everything on the site is protected heritage.
  • Combine Ur with a visit to the Iraq Museum in Baghdad to see the treasures found here.
  • Entry rules for Iraq change from time to time, so check the latest official visa information before you book.
