Few buildings in the Islamic world are as easy to recognise as the Malwiya minaret of Samarra. A tower of golden-brown brick winds upwards in a wide spiral, like a giant seashell standing on the plain beside the Tigris. Visiting the Malwiya minaret in Samarra means standing at the foot of a monument raised more than 1,170 years ago, when this city was the capital of the Abbasid caliphs and one of the largest cities of its time.

In this guide I explain where Samarra is, why the caliphs built a capital here, what makes the spiral minaret and the Great Mosque so special, what else to see in the city, and how to fit Samarra into a journey through Iraq.

Where is the Malwiya minaret?

Samarra lies on the east bank of the Tigris in Salah al-Din province, about 125 km north of Baghdad. By road the drive usually takes roughly two hours depending on traffic, through farmland, palm groves and open steppe. The minaret stands at the northern edge of the modern town, next to the vast brick walls of the Great Mosque of Samarra, and you can see its spiral long before you arrive.

The whole area belongs to the Samarra Archaeological City, which UNESCO added to its World Heritage List in 2007. The ancient city stretched for kilometres along the river, with palaces, mosques, racecourses and gardens, and much of it still lies under the sand as low mounds and outlines.

Samarra, the short-lived capital of the Abbasids

A new city on the Tigris

In 836 the Abbasid caliph al-Mu'tasim moved his court away from Baghdad and founded a new capital at Samarra. Its full name is often explained as Surra man ra'a, "a joy for whoever sees it". For more than half a century, until 892, the caliphs ruled from here, and they filled the city with monumental buildings on a scale that still impresses archaeologists today.

Al-Mutawakkil, the great builder

The caliph who left the strongest mark on Samarra was al-Mutawakkil. Between 848 and 851 he built the Great Mosque, and between 848 and 852 its famous minaret. A few years later, around 859, he added a second large mosque north of the city, today called the Abu Dulaf Mosque. When the caliphs returned to Baghdad, Samarra slowly became a quiet provincial town, which is one reason why so much of its 9th-century layout survived.

The spiral minaret: how it works

The Malwiya is about 52 metres high and roughly 33 metres wide at its base. Its name comes from the Arabic word for "twisted" or "coiled", and many people describe it as a snail shell. A broad ramp runs around the outside of the tower, getting narrower as it climbs, until it reaches a small round pavilion at the top with arched openings.

There is no staircase inside: the spiral ramp is the only way up. The tower was built of brick on a square base and originally stood just outside the mosque, joined to it by a bridge. Scholars still discuss why the architects chose this shape. Some see a memory of the old Mesopotamian ziggurats, the stepped temple towers you can still visit at Ur and near Babylon. Others see a bold statement of the caliph's power, a landmark visible from far across the plain. Either way, nothing quite like it had been built before.

Every time I bring guests to Samarra, the first moment is always the same: the bus stops, everyone looks up, and nobody says a word for a few seconds. In the late afternoon the bricks turn the colour of honey against a deep blue sky. It is my favourite photo stop in all of Iraq. — Saif, your guide

The Great Mosque of Samarra

Once the largest mosque in the world

When it was completed in 851, the Great Mosque of Samarra was the largest mosque in the world. Its outer enclosure covered more than 16 hectares, and historians estimate that it could hold more than 60,000 worshippers. Today you can walk along its massive baked-brick walls, strengthened by 44 semicircular towers, and step through the gateways into the huge open courtyard.

What it looked like inside

Only the outer walls and the minaret have survived, so imagination helps. Sources describe a prayer hall with 17 aisles, 464 pillars, ceilings around 11 metres high and walls decorated with dark blue glass mosaics. Standing in the empty courtyard and picturing that forest of columns gives you a real sense of the scale of Abbasid ambition.

A model for Cairo

The Samarra mosque inspired builders far beyond Iraq. The Mosque of Ibn Tulun in Cairo, built a few decades later, followed it in many respects and even has its own minaret with an outside staircase. If you have visited Cairo, you will recognise the family resemblance immediately.

More to see in Samarra

  • The Abu Dulaf Mosque: about 15 km north of the city, built by al-Mutawakkil around 859. Its spiral minaret is smaller, about 32 metres, and the site is usually much quieter.
  • Remains of the caliphal palaces: brick arches and courtyards that hint at the size of the Abbasid court.
  • The al-Askari shrine: one of the most important holy shrines in Iraq, with a shining golden dome, visited by pilgrims from many countries. It is a place of prayer, so visitors dress modestly and follow the guidance of the shrine staff.

Best time to visit Samarra

The most pleasant months are from October to April, when days are mild and the sky is often clear. Summer in central Iraq is extremely hot, so if you travel then, plan the visit for early morning or late afternoon. For photography, the hour before sunset is ideal: the spiral throws long shadows and the brick glows warm and golden.

Practical tips for your visit

  • Climbing the spiral ramp is not always permitted; access is decided on site, so please follow the instructions of the site staff.
  • If climbing is allowed, wear shoes with good grip: the ramp has no railing and becomes narrow near the top.
  • Bring a hat, sunglasses and water. There is very little shade around the mosque walls.
  • Allow at least one to two hours for the minaret and the Great Mosque, more if you add Abu Dulaf.
  • When visiting the al-Askari shrine, women should bring a headscarf and long loose clothing; men should wear long trousers.
  • Entry rules for Iraq change from time to time, so check the current visa requirements for your nationality before you book.
