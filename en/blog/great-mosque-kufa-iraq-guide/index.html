The Great Mosque of Kufa: history, holy stations and how to visit

One of the oldest mosques in the world, where Imam Ali prayed and judged: history, what to see and practical tips for Kufa.

A short drive from the golden dome of Najaf, the Great Mosque of Kufa rises behind tall, honey-coloured walls. It is one of the oldest mosques in the world, the place where Imam Ali (peace be upon him) prayed, taught and judged during his years in Kufa, and today one of the most visited stops for travellers and pilgrims in southern Iraq. If you plan to visit the Great Mosque of Kufa, this guide explains its history, what to look for inside and how to fit it into an Iraq itinerary.

I bring guests here on almost every trip through the holy cities. Many expect "just another mosque" and leave saying it was the calmest, most moving hour of their journey. Here is why.

Where is the Great Mosque of Kufa?

Kufa lies on the Euphrates in central-southern Iraq, about 10 km northeast of Najaf, in Najaf Province. Today the two cities have almost grown into one another, so the mosque is usually visited on the same day as the Imam Ali Shrine in Najaf. From Baghdad the drive south takes roughly three hours, depending on traffic and checkpoints, and Karbala is about an hour and a half away by road.

The mosque stands in the old heart of Kufa. Around it you will find several smaller shrines and historic houses that belong to the same story, which makes the area perfect to explore on foot.

A short history of one of the oldest mosques in the world

A new city on the Euphrates

Kufa was founded around 638 CE, in the first decades of Islam, as a new settlement on the edge of the desert. According to the historical sources, the mosque was laid out at the same time as the city, under Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas, with the governor's palace, the Dar al-Imara, built right next to it. At first it was a simple open space marked out on the ground; around 670 CE the governor Ziyad rebuilt it in brick and enlarged it, bringing columns from far away.

Older than history, according to tradition

Muslim tradition gives the site a much longer story. Several narrations say that a place of prayer stood here since the time of Prophet Adam, and that Prophet Noah lived nearby and built his ark on this ground. That is why visitors find stations inside the mosque linked to Noah, Abraham and other prophets. Whether you read these traditions as history or as faith, they explain why so many people feel the place is special.

The capital of Imam Ali

The mosque's most important chapter began in 656 CE, when Imam Ali ibn Abi Talib made Kufa the centre of his caliphate. He led prayers in this mosque, gave sermons from it, and sat in judgement among the people. His words on justice, piety and good government, later collected in the famous book Nahj al-Balagha, were delivered in large part in Kufa. In Ramadan of the year 40 AH (early 661 CE), the Imam was struck while leading the dawn prayer in this mosque and passed away two days later. His burial place is in Najaf, where his great shrine stands today.

Later centuries

Kufa remained a great centre of learning for centuries, famous for its grammarians, Qur'an reciters and its angular Kufic script, which takes its name from the city. The mosque was restored many times. The golden dome above the tombs and much of the tilework date to later periods, and the most recent large renovation, completed around 2010, gave the courtyard its gleaming white marble and decorated the prayer halls richly.

What to see inside the Great Mosque of Kufa

The walls and the great courtyard

From outside, the mosque looks almost like a fortress: a nearly square enclosure, around 110 metres on each side, with high walls, round towers and four minarets. Step through the gate and the space opens into a huge, bright courtyard. On a clear evening the marble reflects the sky, and the golden dome and minarets glow in the last light. This is one of the most beautiful sights of any trip to Iraq.

The maqams: stations of prophets and Imams

Scattered across the courtyard are small, covered platforms called maqams, each marking a place connected by tradition to a prophet or an Imam. Among them are the stations of Prophet Abraham, of Prophet Adam, of the angel Gabriel, of Imam Zayn al-Abidin and of Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq. Pilgrims move from one to the next, praying a few moments at each. As a visitor, you are welcome to walk around quietly and watch this gentle rhythm.

The place of Noah's Ark

One corner of the courtyard is linked by tradition to Noah: the place where, it is said, the waters of the flood began and where the ark came to rest. It is a striking spot to hear the story told in the very place it is remembered.

The Dakkat al-Qada and the mihrab of Imam Ali

The Dakkat al-Qada, the "bench of judgement", marks where Imam Ali is said to have heard cases and settled disputes; it is a reminder of his reputation for fairness. Inside the prayer hall, the mihrab where he was leading the prayer when he was struck is the emotional heart of the mosque. Visitors stand here in silence, and many recite prayers for him.

The shrines of Muslim ibn Aqil and Hani ibn Urwa

Attached to the mosque are the domed shrines of Muslim ibn Aqil, the cousin and envoy of Imam Hussein, and of Hani ibn Urwa, the Kufan nobleman who hosted him. Nearby lies the tomb of al-Mukhtar al-Thaqafi. These shrines connect Kufa with the story of Karbala, which many travellers visit next.

Around the mosque: the House of Imam Ali

A few minutes' walk away is the modest house where, according to tradition, Imam Ali lived with his family while in Kufa. Its simplicity, with small rooms and an old well, contrasts strongly with the grandeur of the mosque, and many visitors find it the most touching stop of the day. Close by you can also see the remains of the Dar al-Imara, the governor's palace excavated by archaeologists, and the shrine of Maytham al-Tammar, a companion of Imam Ali. A little outside the centre stands Masjid al-Sahla, another historic mosque of Kufa.

How to get to Kufa

Most travellers reach Kufa from Najaf, which has an international airport and plenty of hotels. A taxi or private car from the Imam Ali Shrine takes about 15–20 minutes. Coming from Baghdad, the road goes south via Hilla, which allows a stop at ancient Babylon on the way. On a guided tour, Kufa, Najaf and Karbala are usually combined over one or two days.

Best time to visit

The most pleasant months are October to April, when days are mild and evenings are cool. The late afternoon is magical: the heat eases, the courtyard fills with families and the marble turns gold at sunset. In summer, plan your visit for early morning or after sunset. Be aware that during major religious occasions Kufa and Najaf receive very large crowds; this can be a powerful experience, but you should plan accommodation well ahead.

Practical tips for visiting the Great Mosque of Kufa

  • Dress modestly: men in long trousers and sleeves; women cover hair, arms and legs. Women are usually asked to wear an abaya, and one can often be borrowed near the entrance.
  • Shoes are left at a shoe depository at the gate; keep your token safe.
  • Bags may be checked at the entrance, so travel light.
  • Photography of the courtyard is generally fine, but be discreet and do not photograph people praying without permission.
  • Walk on the left of praying people, never directly in front of them.
  • Combine the mosque with the House of Imam Ali nearby; allow at least 1.5–2 hours in total.
  • Entry rules for Iraq change from time to time, so check the latest official visa information for your nationality before booking.

Frequently asked questions

Where is the Great Mosque of Kufa?

In the old centre of Kufa, on the Euphrates, about 10 km northeast of Najaf in southern-central Iraq.

Why is the Kufa mosque important?

It is one of the oldest mosques in the world and the place where Imam Ali led prayers, preached and judged while Kufa was his capital.

What are the maqams in the courtyard?

Small covered platforms marking places linked by tradition to prophets and Imams, such as Abraham, Adam, Gabriel and Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq.

Can non-Muslims visit the Great Mosque of Kufa?

Respectful visitors are generally welcomed in the courtyard with modest dress; women are usually asked to wear an abaya. Follow the guidance of the staff.

How long does a visit take?

Allow 1.5 to 2 hours including the nearby House of Imam Ali and the shrine of Muslim ibn Aqil.

Do I need a visa to visit Iraq?

Entry rules change from time to time. Check the latest official information for your nationality before booking your trip.
